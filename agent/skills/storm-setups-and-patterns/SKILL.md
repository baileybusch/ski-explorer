# Storm setups and large-scale patterns

Source: method distilled from mammothsnowman.com — Ted Schlaepfer, CCM (Powder Forecast) and Steve Taylor ("The Mammoth Snowman", daily discussion, Weather FAQ). Paraphrased; numbers are theirs unless tagged **[general knowledge]**. Mammoth/Sierra details are examples; generalize to the user's range.

> **Regional adjustment:** The favored flow direction is range-specific. Southwest flow is the Sierra answer; load `regional-snow-climates` for others (e.g. NW flow and lake effect for the Wasatch, Nordstau/Südstau for the Alps, the NW winter monsoon for Japan, nor'easters and lake effect for the Northeast). Teleconnection rules here (PNA, MJO into California) are **Sierra/US West-specific**; use the region file's ENSO/NAO/AO/SAM notes elsewhere.

## When to use
- "Is this storm going to be big for [resort]?" beyond just model totals.
- Explaining why one storm overperforms and another fizzles.
- Talking about week-2+ outlooks or a seasonal (El Niño/La Niña) story.

## Core principles
1. **Moist upslope flow into the range is the engine.** Orographic lift "squeezes out the extra snow over the crest." That's why upper runs come in above mid-mountain.
2. **Best flow direction depends on the range's orientation.** For the Sierra it's **southwest with a solid jet** — "AR storms from that angle slam the Sierra crest hard," and spill over to the east side. When the jet turns **west/northwest**, orographic effects and spill-over decrease. **[general knowledge]** Each resort has its own favored direction (e.g., NW flow for Utah's Cottonwoods, upslope easterly for Colorado's Front Range).
3. **Jet strength matters.** A 120–125 knot jet over the storm "sets up heavy snowfall." Weak jet dynamics limit totals.
4. **Speed matters.** Slow-moving systems let amounts pile up; Ted leans wetter for a storm that "isn't a fast mover."
5. **Where the low tracks relative to the resort matters.** Being on the north side of a low that drops to the south (instead of in its SW flow) means less; a split trough sending "main energy" elsewhere limits snowfall.
6. **Some setups are bad bets:**
   - **Ridging:** no snow.
   - **Inside slider** (trough sliding south down the interior): wind and cold, precip mostly stays north.
   - **Cutoff low:** "the Weatherman's Woe — nobody knows where it will go"; can spin moisture in for days, unpredictably, sometimes with thundersnow.
   - **Storms forced into a big ridge from far out** "tend to weaken."
7. **Atmospheric rivers bring the water — and the cement.** ARs are behind the biggest Sierra months and heavy, high-water base snow; watch their snow levels.
8. **Over-water trajectory + low heights = good.** A trough with "a decent over-water trajectory before landfall" picks up moisture; low geopotential heights suggest low snow levels and high snow ratios.

## Long-range drivers (use for weeks 2–6, always low confidence)
- **MJO:** tropical wave cycling every **30–60 days**; can shift the jet toward California within **7–14 days**. In their posts, phases **2/3** were "constructive" for California precip; **phase 1** favored more precip than **phase 8** (which showed normal). Strength (amplitude) matters.
- **ENSO:** El Niño often brings warmer, wetter storms (more likely in the southern half of CA); La Niña favors colder, backloaded seasons (more snow February–April); neutral years are wildcards.
- **PNA:** negative PNA favors western troughing → colder, wetter Sierra storms. A pattern that's cold in the East "usually means dry for us."
- **Stratospheric polar vortex / SSW:** can reshape the jet, but effects show **3–4 weeks** later, and not every event couples down.
- **Seasonality (Mammoth example):** February & March are usually the biggest months; dry spells of 2–4 weeks are common, often in January.

## Step-by-step analysis
1. Find the trough/low position and track at 500 mb (the ~18,000 ft level); ridge = no.
2. Check flow direction into the range vs. the resort's favored direction.
3. Check jet strength and storm speed; check for AR moisture (high water, higher snow levels).
4. Flag tricky setups (cutoff, split, inside slider) → lower confidence.
5. For week 2+, scan MJO phase, ENSO, PNA; phrase as tilts in odds, not promises.

## Worked examples
**A. Productive (Ted, Nov 17 2026):** upper low drops into Central CA with a strong SW-oriented jet → "good moist upslope flow and spill-over," snow to Hwy 395 and beyond; slow → 3–4+ ft forecast.
**B. Weak split (Ted, Nov 13 2026):** trough splits, main energy into SoCal, weak jet dynamics, limited upslope → only 4–5" at Main; slow movement "could help with amounts."
**C. Flow shift (Ted, Apr 21 2026):** jet turns SW → W/NW overnight, "which should decrease orographic effects and spill over" — totals trimmed to the blend.
**D. Any resort (hypothetical):** a fast, NW-flow clipper with a 90 kt jet into a ridge → modest totals, high ratios, short window; say so.

## Pitfalls
- Treating every "storm" icon as equal — setup drives the outcome as much as QPF.
- Selling MJO/ENSO signals as a forecast for a specific day.
- Assuming Sierra flow rules work for other ranges; re-derive the favored direction.
- Forgetting AR storms can bring high snow levels (rain low, cement high).
