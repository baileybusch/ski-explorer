# Pacific Northwest + BC Coast (Cascades, Coast Mountains)

Tags: **[src]** = linked source; **[GK]** = general knowledge, approximate.

## Climate type
- **Maritime/coastal**: the Washington Cascades are Avalanche.org's example of maritime climate [src]. Mild, cloudy, frequent storms, heavy precipitation that can mix rain and snow [src].

## Snow-to-liquid and density
- Typically dense: think ~7:1 to 12:1 in most storms, with lighter snow (15:1+) mostly in cold post-frontal showers or with Arctic outflow [GK, approx.].
- Cascade snow is wetter than Utah's [src: Steenburgh via KUTV].

## Storm setups and flow
- Main engine: **westerly to southwesterly Pacific flow** into north–south ranges; strong orographic lift on the west slopes [GK].
- Atmospheric rivers bring huge water, often with high freezing levels; "Pineapple Express" events can bring heavy mountain snow that melts fast in the warmth [src: Wikipedia, Pineapple Express].
- **Post-frontal NW flow** with cold air: showery, lighter snow, often the best powder [GK].
- **Puget Sound Convergence Zone**: flow splits around the Olympics and converges over Puget Sound, producing a narrow band of heavy precip, often favoring Stevens Pass/Snoqualmie area [src: Wikipedia PSCZ for mechanism; resort effect GK].
- **Cold easterly gap flow** (Snoqualmie/Stevens, Fraser outflow) holds cold air at pass level so storms start as snow; it can bring freezing rain when it erodes [GK].

## Snow-killers
- Warm ARs, rising freezing levels, rain to summit at lower areas [src: Wikipedia AR/Pineapple Express; GK].
- Rain-on-snow followed by refreeze forms crusts [GK].
- Strong wind on exposed terrain; low visibility and fog [GK].

## Elevation and freezing levels
- Low bases (Mt. Baker base 3,500 ft, top 5,089 ft) [src: Wikipedia]; Whistler spans a large vertical from valley to alpine [GK].
- Freezing level is the main forecast variable; storms commonly see it swing from ~1,000 m to well above 2,000 m [GK, approx.]. Upper mountain can be powder while the base gets rain.

## Seasonality
- Peak snowfall roughly Dec–Feb, with deep spring snowpacks [GK].
- Huge totals: Mt. Baker holds the US single-season record of 1,140 in (1998–99) [src: Wikipedia, Thompson Pass article]; Whistler recorded 1,432 cm in 2009–10 [src: Wikipedia, Whistler Blackcomb].

## Powder-day profile
- Deep but often heavy; best days come when cold air follows a wet storm (dense base, light top) [GK; matches the "right-side-up" principle in Steenburgh & Alcott 2008].
- Check freezing level vs. base and mid-mountain before calling it; tree skiing helps in low visibility [GK].

## ENSO and teleconnections
- La Niña tends to bring wetter, colder winters to the Pacific Northwest; El Niño tends warmer and drier [src: NOAA climate.gov ENSO page].
- Negative PNA favors western troughing/cold; positive PNA ridge often means dry [src: NOAA CPC PNA; GK].

## Avalanche and snowpack
- Maritime: deep, homogeneous snowpack; fewer persistent weak layers; danger spikes with storms and drops fast [src: Avalanche.org]. Wet slides with warm-ups and rain [GK].

## Example resorts
- **Mt. Baker (WA)**: record snowfall, low elevation [src].
- **Whistler Blackcomb (BC)**: big vertical, freezing-level-sensitive [src/GK].
- **Crystal Mountain (WA)**: Washington's largest; benefits from cold east-side air [GK].
